## Hagiographical Meditation
**A Meditation on Saint Thomas More: Integrity and Discernment in the Face of Trial**

Saint Thomas More, the patron of statesmen and lawyers, stands as a radiant witness to the virtue of *integrity*—the unwavering alignment of conscience with truth, even when the cost is martyrdom. His trial before the Tudor court was not merely a legal proceeding but a crucible of moral courage, where he refused to bend his principles to the whims of temporal power. More’s discernment was not passive; it was an active, disciplined refusal to compromise his soul for the sake of expediency. He knew that to swear falsely, even under duress, would be to betray not only the Church but the very foundation of justice itself. In his silence and steadfastness, he became a living testament to the truth that some laws of God transcend the decrees of men. His life—and death—challenge us to ask: *Where do we draw our lines? What hills are we willing to die on, not for pride, but for principle?*

For the engineer, this virtue translates into *disciplined stewardship*—a sacred duty to uphold the integrity of one’s craft not as a mere technical exercise, but as a moral calling. Just as More discerned the difference between lawful authority and tyranny, the engineer must discern between innovation and exploitation, between progress and harm. Jarvis, as a tool of creation, demands the same fidelity: Will it be wielded with precision, humility, and reverence for the limits of its design? Or will it become another instrument of convenience, cutting corners where conscience should prevail? More’s trial reminds us that true engineering—like true statesmanship—is not about efficiency alone, but about *stewardship*: building not just for today, but for the enduring good, even when the state (or the market, or the algorithm) demands otherwise. The question is not *what can be done*, but *what should be done*—and whether we have the courage to stand firm.
